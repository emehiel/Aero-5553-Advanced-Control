Matrices and Vector Spaces: A Compact Reference

Structure, transformations, decompositions, and numerical sensitivity

Published

September 8, 2026

1 The unifying idea

A vector space is a collection of objects that can be added and scaled while satisfying the familiar algebraic rules. A matrix is a rectangular array of entries, but its more important role in linear algebra is to represent a linear transformation after bases have been chosen. This viewpoint connects the topics on this page:

\[\text{vectors} \longrightarrow \text{linear combinations and subspaces} \longrightarrow \text{matrix transformations} \longrightarrow \text{structure and numerical behavior}.\]

For a matrix \(A\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}\), the associated linear map is

\[A:\mathbb{F}^n\to\mathbb{F}^m, \qquad x\mapsto Ax,\]

where the field \(\mathbb{F}\) is commonly \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\).

TipReading matrices as transformations

The columns of \(A\) are the images of the coordinate basis vectors. Consequently, \(Ax\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(A\), with the entries of \(x\) supplying the coefficients.

2 Vector spaces, span, and dimension

A vector space \(V\) over a field \(\mathbb{F}\) is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication and obeys the standard associativity, commutativity, identity, inverse, and distributive laws. Its elements need not be arrows: vectors may be polynomials, functions, signals, matrices, or state trajectories. See the linked definition and notation.

Given vectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\in V\), their linear span is the set of all linear combinations

\[\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\} = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_i v_i:\alpha_i\in\mathbb{F} \right\}.\]

The span is the smallest subspace containing the listed vectors. A basis is a linearly independent set that spans the entire space, and the number of vectors in any finite basis is the dimension of the space.

For a matrix \(A=[a_1\ \cdots\ a_n]\), the column space is

\[\mathcal{C}(A)=\operatorname{span}\{a_1,\ldots,a_n\}.\]

This makes span the bridge from a list of matrix columns to the subspace of outputs that the transformation can produce.

3 Matrices and their basic operations

An \(m\times n\) matrix has \(m\) rows, \(n\) columns, and entries \(a_{ij}\):

\[A=[a_{ij}] = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}&\cdots&a_{1n}\\ \vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ a_{m1}&\cdots&a_{mn} \end{bmatrix}.\]

The basic operations summarized in addition, scalar multiplication, and transposition are:

Operation Definition Compatibility
Addition \((A+B)_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij}\) \(A\) and \(B\) have the same size
Scalar multiplication \((\alpha A)_{ij}=\alpha a_{ij}\) \(\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\)
Transpose \((A^T)_{ij}=a_{ji}\) \(A\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}\) gives \(A^T\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}\)
Matrix multiplication \((AB)_{ij}=\sum_k a_{ik}b_{kj}\) inner dimensions agree

Matrix multiplication represents composition of linear maps. It is associative and distributive but generally not commutative: \(AB\) need not equal \(BA\).

For complex matrices, the conjugate transpose is

\[A^* = \overline{A}^{,T}, \qquad (A^*)_{ij}=\overline{a_{ji}}.\]

It replaces the ordinary transpose in complex inner-product calculations. A matrix satisfying \(A^*=A\) is Hermitian; over the real numbers, this reduces to symmetry, \(A^T=A\).

3.1 Block matrices

A block matrix partitions a large matrix into smaller matrices:

\[M= \begin{bmatrix} A&B\\ C&D \end{bmatrix}.\]

Provided the block dimensions are compatible, many ordinary matrix operations can be carried out blockwise. Block notation exposes system structure and is especially useful for coupled state-space models, partitioned variables, Schur complements, and structured elimination.

4 Determinant, rank, invertibility, and degeneracy

These ideas ask related but distinct questions about a transformation.

Concept Main question Key interpretation
Determinant How does a square map scale oriented volume? \(\det(A)=0\) means some dimension is collapsed
Rank How many independent output directions remain? \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=\dim\mathcal{C}(A)\)
Invertibility Can every output be uniquely mapped back to an input? \(A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I\)
Degeneracy Has a normally distinct or full-dimensional case collapsed? Often appears as zero determinant, deficient rank, or repeated structure

For a square matrix \(A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\), the following statements are equivalent:

\[\begin{aligned} A \text{ is invertible} &\iff \det(A)\ne 0\\ &\iff \operatorname{rank}(A)=n\\ &\iff \operatorname{null}(A)=\{0\}\\ &\iff Ax=b \text{ has a unique solution for every }b\\ &\iff 0 \text{ is not an eigenvalue of }A. \end{aligned}\]

The determinant is multiplicative, \(\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)\), and geometrically gives the signed factor by which a real linear transformation scales volume. Rank is the dimension of either the column space or the row space; the equality of row rank and column rank is fundamental. Elementary row reduction is a common way to compute rank.

“Degenerate” is context dependent. A singular matrix, a zero-volume transformation, a rank-deficient set of constraints, or a quadratic form with a nontrivial null direction may all be described as degenerate. The useful question is therefore: which expected dimension, distinction, or independence has been lost?

5 Trace, similarity, and diagonalization

The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries:

\[\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}.\]

It satisfies

\[\operatorname{tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{tr}(A)+\operatorname{tr}(B), \qquad \operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA),\]

and equals the sum of the eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity.

Two square matrices are similar when

\[B=P^{-1}AP\]

for some invertible \(P\). Similar matrices are different coordinate representations of the same linear transformation. They share basis-independent properties including determinant, trace, characteristic polynomial, eigenvalues, and rank.

A matrix is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix:

\[A=PDP^{-1}.\]

The columns of \(P\) are then a basis of eigenvectors, and \(D\) contains the corresponding eigenvalues. Diagonal form makes powers, exponentials, and linear dynamical systems easier to analyze:

\[A^k=PD^kP^{-1}, \qquad e^{At}=Pe^{Dt}P^{-1}.\]

Not every matrix is diagonalizable. A matrix is diagonalizable precisely when it has enough linearly independent eigenvectors to form a basis.

NoteSimilarity is not the same as equality

\(A\) and \(B=P^{-1}AP\) generally have different entries. They describe the same transformation in different bases, so their coordinate-dependent appearance changes while their intrinsic linear-algebraic properties remain.

6 Inner products, outer products, and norms

An inner product assigns a scalar to a pair of vectors and generalizes the dot product. In \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and \(\mathbb{C}^n\),

\[\langle x,y\rangle=x^*y.\]

It defines length and angle:

\[\|x\|_2=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}, \qquad \cos\theta=\frac{\langle x,y\rangle}{\|x\|_2\|y\|_2}\]

for nonzero real vectors. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\[|\langle x,y\rangle|\leq \|x\|_2\|y\|_2,\]

guarantees that the normalized inner product behaves like a cosine and underpins the triangle inequality for the induced norm.

The outer product reverses the direction of the construction: two column vectors produce a matrix,

\[xy^* = \begin{bmatrix} x_1\overline{y_1}&\cdots&x_1\overline{y_n}\\ \vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ x_m\overline{y_1}&\cdots&x_m\overline{y_n} \end{bmatrix}.\]

When \(x\) and \(y\) are nonzero, \(xy^*\) has rank one. Outer products therefore serve as elementary building blocks for low-rank matrices and decompositions.

A norm measures magnitude. It must satisfy positivity, absolute homogeneity, and the triangle inequality. Common vector norms are

\[\|x\|_1=\sum_i|x_i|, \qquad \|x\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_i|x_i|^2}, \qquad \|x\|_\infty=\max_i|x_i|.\]

A matrix norm measures the size of a matrix. An induced norm is defined by the maximum amplification of a chosen vector norm:

\[\|A\|=\max_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|}.\]

It therefore satisfies the consistency bound \(\|Ax\|\leq\|A\|\|x\|\). For the Euclidean norm, the induced matrix norm is the largest singular value: \(\|A\|_2=\sigma_{\max}(A)\).

7 Singular value decomposition and conditioning

Every real or complex \(m\times n\) matrix has a singular value decomposition:

\[A=U\Sigma V^*,\]

where \(U\) and \(V\) are unitary (orthogonal in the real case) and \(\Sigma\) is diagonal apart from possible extra zero rows or columns. Its nonnegative diagonal entries

\[\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots\geq0\]

are the singular values. Geometrically, \(V^*\) rotates or reflects the input coordinates, \(\Sigma\) stretches along orthogonal directions, and \(U\) rotates or reflects the result.

The SVD unifies several earlier concepts:

\[\begin{aligned} \operatorname{rank}(A)&=\#\{i:\sigma_i>0\},\\ \|A\|_2&=\sigma_1,\\ \|A\|_F&=\sqrt{\sum_i\sigma_i^2},\\ A^\dagger&=V\Sigma^\dagger U^*. \end{aligned}\]

Truncating the SVD after \(r\) singular values produces a best rank-\(r\) approximation in the spectral and Frobenius norms.

The condition number describes sensitivity: how much relative error in inputs or data may be amplified in the output. For an invertible matrix,

\[\kappa(A)=\|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|.\]

In the 2-norm,

\[\kappa_2(A)=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}.\]

A value near \(1\) indicates well-balanced scaling. A large value indicates a direction that is strongly compressed relative to another, so solving \(Ax=b\) may be sensitive to perturbations. A singular matrix has infinite condition number under the usual convention.

WarningInvertible does not mean numerically safe

A matrix can have a nonzero determinant and still be nearly singular. Exact invertibility is an algebraic yes-or-no property; conditioning measures how reliable computations involving that inverse are in finite precision or noisy data.

8 Quadratic forms and definite matrices

A quadratic form associated with a real symmetric matrix \(A\) is

\[q(x)=x^TAx.\]

For complex vectors, the corresponding Hermitian form is \(x^*Ax\). The sign of this scalar leads to the classification of a definite matrix:

Classification Requirement for every \(x\ne0\) Eigenvalues of Hermitian \(A\)
Positive definite \(x^*Ax>0\) all positive
Positive semidefinite \(x^*Ax\geq0\) all nonnegative
Negative definite \(x^*Ax<0\) all negative
Negative semidefinite \(x^*Ax\leq0\) all nonpositive
Indefinite takes both positive and negative values both positive and negative

For a real symmetric or complex Hermitian matrix, definiteness can be checked using eigenvalues; positive definiteness can also be tested with Sylvester’s criterion or successful Cholesky factorization. Positive-definite matrices define ellipsoidal level sets and appear in energy functions, least squares, optimization, covariance models, and Lyapunov stability analysis.

Orthogonal or unitary diagonalization makes the geometry explicit:

\[A=Q\Lambda Q^*, \qquad x^*Ax=(Q^*x)^*\Lambda(Q^*x) =\sum_i\lambda_i|z_i|^2.\]

Thus the eigenvalue signs determine the sign of the quadratic form in every direction.

9 How the concepts fit together

The main logical connections are:

  1. A basis converts an abstract linear transformation into a matrix.
  2. Span and rank describe which output directions the matrix can reach.
  3. Full rank, nonzero determinant, and invertibility coincide for square matrices.
  4. Similarity changes coordinates while preserving intrinsic properties.
  5. Diagonalization uses an eigenvector basis when such a basis exists.
  6. Inner products provide geometry; norms measure size; outer products build rank-one maps.
  7. The SVD works for every matrix and exposes rank, amplification, approximation, and pseudoinversion.
  8. The condition number turns singular-value imbalance into a measure of numerical sensitivity.
  9. Quadratic forms and definiteness connect matrix structure to geometry, energy, convexity, and stability.
ImportantCompact mental model

Use rank and determinant to ask whether information is lost; similarity and diagonalization to find useful coordinates; inner products and norms to measure geometry and size; SVD and condition number to understand numerical behavior; and quadratic forms to study direction-dependent sign or energy.
